# Electrical engineering fundamentals

Ohm's law states that voltage equals current times resistance: V = I × R.
Current in amperes equals voltage divided by resistance: I = V / R.
Resistance in ohms equals voltage divided by current: R = V / I.

Electrical power in watts equals voltage times current: P = V × I.
Power can also be computed as P = I² × R or P = V² / R.
Electrical energy is power multiplied by time; one kilowatt-hour is 1000
watts used for one hour, or 3.6 megajoules.

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points,
measured in volts. Current is the flow of electric charge, measured in
amperes; one ampere is one coulomb of charge per second. Resistance
opposes current flow and is measured in ohms.

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) says the sum of currents entering a node
equals the sum of currents leaving it. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)
says the sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero.

Resistors in series add directly: Rtotal = R1 + R2 + R3. Resistors in
parallel combine as the reciprocal of the sum of reciprocals: for two
resistors, Rtotal = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2). Two equal resistors in
parallel give half the resistance of one.

Capacitors combine the opposite way to resistors: capacitors in parallel
add directly, while capacitors in series combine like parallel resistors.

DC (direct current) flows in one direction, like from a battery. AC
(alternating current) reverses direction periodically, like mains power.
For a sine wave, the RMS voltage equals the peak voltage divided by the
square root of 2, about 0.707 times the peak. UK and EU mains is about
230 volts RMS at 50 hertz; US mains is about 120 volts RMS at 60 hertz.

A voltage divider made of two resistors gives an output voltage of
Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2), where R2 is the resistor the output is
taken across. Voltage dividers are for signals and references, not for
powering loads, because the load changes the ratio.

Conventional current flows from positive to negative, although electrons
actually move the opposite way. A short circuit is an unintended
low-resistance path that causes excessive current. An open circuit is a
break in the path so no current flows.
